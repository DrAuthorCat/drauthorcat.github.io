The Taste Of Death 
(Close)

The storm had not waned for hours, and the muddy ground had become like a great mirror, showing only the reflection of the gray evening sky. The sun was behind clouds of grey; its warmth had long been forgotten. The castle of Abraham stood strong underneath the storm; its gray walls, now damp and black, were like frozen waves upon the fields of mud. They stood above the army that surrounded it now; soldiers' eyes glinted with fear. And while the walls reached tall, in truth, the castle was but a glorified watchtower, with only a hall and a food store. The army surrounding it had made all manner of tents and shelters, and dashed throughout their encampment; the smoke of campfires drifted above them. There was laughter, and the men rolled dice and sharpened their blades. The army was there for one reason: to take back their castle from the invaders from the east.  

But within Castle Abraham, there were only the sounds of the crows and the gentle patter of the rain. The great hall was no different; in silence sat the new lord Alain and the five counselors that he’d brought on his campaign. They wore their armor, always ready. The silver of their plate twinkled with candlelight. The hall was older than any of them, made of black stone; the few windows it had were accented with weathered wood and closed tightly.  

Alain's brown eyes had drifted away in thought, and now he only dreamed of a memory once forgotten. He was a boy again, watching as his father had left for war. He remembered the warmth of the summer sun, of the tears his mother had shed that he had not understood. But now he had. He recalled the waiting, the endless nights, and the dreamless sleep. He wondered when he’d stopped waiting for his father’s return; he wondered if he ever would.  

He was awoken from thought by his Upper Council Marcel, as he broke the silence by clearing his throat. 

“My lord,” he said, his voice as light as a feather. “If I may,” Alain nodded, and the man continued, “I fear we’ve been betrayed.” 

“And so it seems.” Alain grinned in the slightest; his teeth were like gold in the shimmering candlelight, and his eyes still looked elsewhere, to a place far from here.  

“We must do something,” Marcel said. And soon the hall erupted into chatter. 

Alain paid the room no mind as his hand groped for a beard that wasn't there. He’d had it cut before this campaign, and now only a hint of it had returned. He sighed.   

“Quiet,” he said, but the hall only grew louder. “Quiet!” Everyone turned to face him; some forced a face of composure, but most could not; they wore the face of scared children.

“Yes, we’ve been abandoned.” He stood from his chair, a bear coat dragged on the floor behind him as he paced, the ends of it weathered and dirty. “We are alone and surrounded, but we are still alive. Hal and his great army have more than enough men to take this place by force, yet still we are alive. They’ve the first move, and they’ve made it, and so now we wait.”

“We can't wait forever,” Marcel said, and as the room became quiet, his eyes shot around, and Alain noticed a fear in him that he’d never seen before; his brown eyes had a glint to them, and his forehead shone with sweat. And the man could not hold his gaze.

“Each day we wait, the invasion moves forward; surely they can not wait here while their homeland burns elsewhere,” Alain said. “It is only a matter of time before Hal’s bannermen abandon him to fight us alone.” The hall flowed with whispers, and Alain suddenly felt fatigue strike him.
