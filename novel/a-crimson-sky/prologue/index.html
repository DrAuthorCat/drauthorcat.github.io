A Crimson Sky

Prologue

The prologue to A Crimson Sky by Josiah A. Ferrer.



Fifteen walked the red dunes, their cloaks whipping to a chaotic drum. Even with Maja almost fully behind the mountains to the west, it still painted the world in a hot reddish haze. Before the group was a wall with a great gate, so tall that the men who walked along it were like distant specks. Areala looked up, knowing those distant specs had spotted them long before they’d seen them. There was a silence between the fifteen. The only sound was the howling wind that brought no respite from the heat, and the whispers of cloaks. Many looked back, watching the setting Maja, their eyes entranced. Others watched the horizon, their hands growing restless as they adjusted the grips on their spears. Areala looked back, watching as Maja slowly sank beneath the horizon, her gut filled with dread, and she suddenly felt the way her shirt stuck to her back, the way her cloak scratched against her neck. And for a moment, they all held their breath. Then, the great gate began to open with a roar as it slid against the sand, rumbling the very earth, and as one, they finally breathed. 

The group walked in with a quickness to their steps. Surrounding them was a great inner gate, walls on all sides. There were countless guardsmen, with their white cloaks and leather tunics; most had their helmets tied to their belts, and their spears held tightly at their sides. Soon the great gate began to close, slowly and with the same rumble. Some of the group looked back, but Areala didn't; she never did. And when it finally came to a close, Areala allowed herself a moment of relief; she was safe. It was the same process as it had always been: the guardsmen looked over their equipment and goods, of which they had none, and, after an unnecessarily long process, the inner gate was opened; they walked into God’s Step, the first city of The Waste. Before them lay the outer city, small houses and huts, and a few tents. In the distance, they could see the rest, the white adobe buildings and long towers that reached up to suck in air for cooling, the different districts, and on the coast, The Step: the house of power for all things here, the hand of The Coalition.

She paid it only a glance; the real view was the sea, it reached on into the horizon, and right now she dreamt of drowning in it, but she knew the water was no escape, that even the sea was a warm hell. As she took in the view, she could feel her body accepting the fatigue of her journey; she wanted to collapse. She wanted to throw down her spear and lie beneath the nearest shadow, but she wouldn't. 

“We should have stayed in Koth,” Jaiib said.

 He was older than the rest, but his stance was just as youthful. His face was hanging down, and his skin looked rough to the touch, but his beard held a strong black with no signs of graying, and while his hair was also a strong black, it was thinned and whipped in the wind. “By Maja, this was too close, even for me. It wasn't worth it,” he said, and everyone’s eyes went to Areala, waiting for her response.

 She wanted to sigh, but she didn’t; she had to play mother for a while longer. She was older than most of the group, but not as old as Jaiib. Her face showed its age beneath her eyes; she had a sharp chin and a coldness to her hazel brown eyes. A scar swiped across her right cheek and led up into the bottom of her ear. She could feel it now on her face, like the dead skin on a snake, pulsing, thumping. 

“We’re home. Alive and well. Let’s forget about that godforsaken waste, and if you must argue, let it be out of this heat.” She said, her face showed no expression, but her voice held a lightness to it, a lightness she did not intend. 

“And what if? What if they didn't open the gate? What roof would we argue under?” He said, hands raised and looking around to his fellow men. But none raised a voice to him. Their faces held low, waiting, watching. 

“What of, what ifs? We made it. That, is the end of it.” She said, her voice became louder as she spoke. 

“You are burden, Kal sul.‘you will be the death of us.’” Jaiib said, simple and clear.

She wanted to let it be; she wanted to ignore him, but she couldn't. It was the heat she thought, but regardless of the reason, an anger overtook her. She had no words for him; it was a swift, spiteful anger. A question: how could he allow this pettiness, this emotion, to lead to death? There was a chance, perhaps he would see reason. But such an insult could be met with only one response; she raised her spear and slammed it into the ground twice. The rest of the men backed away, forming a circle around them. Whispers flowed through the crowd, eyes all watching Jaiib. Jaiib’s face flickered; he took a step back, threw his spear to the ground and drew his saber from beneath his cloak. The world darkened as Maja slid beneath the distant mountains. The guards watched in silence. 

“Leave here, and return to your family. I give you this one gift.” She said, but she knew his response before he spoke. The old man was stubborn; she knew he wouldn't give in. But would he really die? Die, just to see me dead. My brother, my son. She had no grief. 

“You will be the death of them,” he said,

He was the first to strike; he dashed forward, dust following his trail. There was a flash of sparks as she deflected his strike and stepped back, maintaining her distance. She’d seen him fight before; his hidden speed was no surprise. But he didn't stop, another slash and another, she tried to keep her distance, but he seemed to close in, inch by inch. He moved like a beast, and never before had she felt the strength of his blows. She’d seen him kill, but she hadn't known the force he’d held; with each deflection, her whole body rang like a church bell. It had only been a few minutes, but she could already feel herself grow tired. She kept her focus, but soon the movements became like a dance; her body responded, and all she could hear was the clash of metal, followed by a flash. Until she raised her spear tip to deflect, but no attack came, he had broken the rhythm and within a second was past her spear. 

He was fast, but so was she. She dropped her spear and jumped back a breath before his blade sliced through the air in front of her. She drew her saber and stood at the ready. She knew that if she remained defensive, the fight would be his, but something told her a trap was set and ready; she felt it.

She was tired, tired from the trip, tired from this fight, and for a moment she was tired of life, of this game and of this heat. While she braced for his attack, none came; he circled her slowly, and she took this time to observe him. He didn't seem to be breathing heavily; he held his blade at the ready. She wondered if he was even human. He even stepped with no pattern; there was no opening. Everyone’s eyes followed them as they walked in a circle. She was getting tired; it was now or never. She lunged forward and thrust her blade, and then the trap sprung. He dodged with a step to the left and kicked up sand into the air, making a cloud of dust. It wasn't enough to hide him entirely, but it was enough for her to lose sight of him for just a moment. Through the dust came his saber; the blade was coming right for her stomach, her blade was too forward to deflect in time, and he was too close. With no thought, she lifted her right arm and blocked the blow. His blade pierced her hand. She could see the tip as it pushed through. She pushed with all her might and managed to redirect it before it pierced her chest. In the same move, she brought her saber down on his neck with a slash, but she only cut through the dust, and as it cleared. She could see he’d dropped his blade and was now safely a few feet away. She pulled his blade from her hand and threw it to the ground behind her. 

“Enough, Jaiib. I will not kill a man with no weapon.” She said, but he spoke no response, and she could see him pick up his dropped spear from the ground. There was a hint of a smile on her face. With the beat of her heart, Areala could feel the blood spurt from her hand, spilling down to the ground. Even now, she felt a faint weakness, as if from her hand dripped her very soul. She knew then it would only get worse. The smile overtook her face. She should’ve surrendered while she had her life still intact; this fight was over, but she didn't. Not because she knew she could win, not because she wanted to win, but rather, she was starting to have fun.
